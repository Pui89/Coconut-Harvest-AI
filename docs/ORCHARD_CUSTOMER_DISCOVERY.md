# Coconut Orchard Customer-Discovery Guide

This guide supports low-cost, early conversations about whether a photo-based orchard survey and harvest-planning assistant solves a real problem. It is a research instrument, not proof of demand, a sales promise, or a commitment to deploy a robot.

## What to test first

**Working hypothesis:** some coconut growers or agricultural advisers spend meaningful time inspecting palms, counting visible bunches, planning harvest visits, and recording results. A human-reviewed photo workflow might reduce record-keeping effort or improve consistency.

This is only a hypothesis. Do not assume that growers have this problem, want AI, can provide usable photos, or would pay for a tool.

## Who to interview

Aim for 8–12 short interviews as an initial discovery target, if access is available:
- coconut growers or orchard managers;
- agricultural extension officers or cooperative staff;
- researchers who study coconut production or harvest operations;
- equipment or farm-service providers familiar with the workflow.

Do not describe this target as completed research until interviews have actually taken place.

## 15-minute interview script

### Opening (1 minute)
"I'm exploring ways to make coconut-orchard inspection and harvest planning easier. I am researching current practices, not selling a finished product. May I take anonymous notes? I will not publish your name, farm location, photos, or other identifying details without separate permission."

### Current workflow (5 minutes)
1. Please walk me through how you inspect coconut palms and decide what work is needed.
2. How do you currently count or record visible bunches and track which palms need attention?
3. How often do you do this, and roughly how long does it take for a typical area?
4. Which parts are most difficult: access, visibility, labour availability, records, timing, safety, or something else?
5. What happens when a count or harvest plan is wrong? Can you describe a recent example?

### Existing alternatives and value (4 minutes)
6. What tools, people, or records do you use now? What works well about them?
7. Have you tried to improve this process before? What happened?
8. Which measurable outcome would matter most: time saved, fewer missed bunches, better records, lower costs, or another result?
9. What would make a photo-based tool impractical—for example, connectivity, phone/camera access, image quality, privacy, or extra work?

### Concept test (3 minutes)
Only after hearing their current workflow, briefly describe the proposed **early** product: "A local-first tool to review permitted orchard photos, mark visible bunches, let a person correct the count, and export a simple inspection report. It would not autonomously instruct a robot to cut or harvest."

10. Which part, if any, would fit your workflow?
11. What evidence would you need before trying it on a small, supervised sample?
12. Who would need to approve a trial, and what data or safety restrictions would apply?

### Close (2 minutes)
13. Would you be willing to review a paper mock-up or discuss a small, no-obligation test later? It is fine to say no.
14. Is there another person with direct experience whom I should ask?
15. May I contact you again? Record contact details separately and only with permission.

## Evidence and ethics rules

- Ask about recent real behaviour before asking whether someone "likes AI."
- Record disconfirming feedback, including no problem, no interest, and existing tools that work well.
- Separate participant statements, your interpretation, and assumptions.
- Do not invent interview counts, quotes, time savings, willingness to pay, or customer commitments.
- Do not collect personal, sensitive, or identifiable farm data unless necessary and explicitly agreed; do not publish raw interview notes.
- Ask separately for permission before photographing people, entering a farm, collecting orchard imagery, or reusing contributed data. Permission to view an image is not automatically permission to publish, train on, or redistribute it.
- Do not promise accuracy, ROI, autonomous harvesting, physical safety, or a delivery date.
- If a participant expresses interest, agree on a small next step in writing and define data rights, responsibilities, and success measures before any pilot.

## Decision rule after the first interviews

Summarise evidence rather than relying on enthusiasm. Consider moving to a mock-up or workflow test only if several participants independently describe a similar, consequential problem and can explain how they currently handle it. If needs differ or current methods work well, revise the hypothesis rather than forcing the product idea.

The product remains an early software prototype. Customer discovery does not validate the detector or prove commercial demand; detector performance requires a separate, rights-cleared, held-out benchmark.
